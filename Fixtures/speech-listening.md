# A morning at the harbour market

The harbour market opens at six, an hour before the first ferry leaves. Most stalls are still covered when the bakers arrive with their carts, and the smell of warm bread reaches the water before the light does. By half past six the fish sellers have laid out their ice, the flower stall has filled its buckets, and someone has started the coffee machine that always hisses a little too loudly. Nobody seems to mind. It is the sound that tells the street the day has begun.

Marta has run the vegetable stall for nineteen years. She keeps a small notebook in her apron pocket, and in it she writes the price of everything she buys from the farms in the valley. On a good week she sells about 340 kilograms of potatoes, 85 kilograms of tomatoes and more onions than she would like to count. She says the numbers matter less than the faces. "If the same people come back every Saturday," she says, "then I am doing something right."

The ferry to the island takes forty minutes when the sea is calm. In winter it can take an hour, and on the worst days it does not run at all. The timetable is printed on a board beside the ticket office, and it has not changed since 2019: departures at 7:10, 9:40, 12:15, 15:30 and 18:05. Tickets cost 6.50 for adults and 3.25 for children, and dogs travel free if they stay on the lower deck.

## Notes from the workshop

Behind the market there is a small workshop where two friends repair old radios. Their bench is covered with valves, screws, coils of wire and a magnifying lamp that has lost one of its arms. They keep a list of every set they have repaired in a plain text file called repairs.txt, and they back it up every Friday. When a customer asks how long a repair will take, they always answer the same way: about two weeks, unless the radio has opinions of its own.

Their favourite tools are simple ones:

- a soldering iron with a fine tip
- a set of small screwdrivers with worn handles
- a multimeter that has survived three falls from the bench
- a notebook for the wiring diagrams they draw by hand

Last month they restored a radio built in 1957. It had spent decades in an attic, and the cabinet smelled of dust and pine. When they switched it on for the first time, it hummed for a long moment and then found a station playing an old waltz. They both laughed, because it seemed to them that the radio had chosen the music itself.

## A short technical interlude

Some of the younger stallholders now take card payments with a small reader that connects to a phone. One of them, a student who studies computer science at the university, explained how the reader works while she waited for customers. The phone talks to the bank over HTTPS, she said, and the receipts are stored in a file called payments.csv that she exports at the end of each day. She keeps her project notes on a website at https://www.example.com/market-notes, and she uses an API from a company whose name she could never pronounce.

When something goes wrong, she opens a terminal on her laptop and runs a short check:

```
$ make verify
CHECK whitespace
CHECK core-test-debug
PASS: 8 checks
```

"If it says pass," she explained, "I go back to selling honey. If it says fail, I make more coffee and read the error from the top." She says the first line of an error is usually the honest one, and the rest is the computer apologising.

## The afternoon

By noon the market is crowded. Families walk slowly between the stalls, children ask for samples of cheese, and the fishmonger tells the same joke about the one that got away to everyone who will listen. The sun moves across the square, and the shade from the old plane trees shifts from the bakery to the bookshop. The bookshop owner sets out a table of second hand paperbacks, each one priced at two euros, and leaves a jar for the money because, as he says, honest people deserve a little trust.

At three o'clock the wind usually turns. It comes in from the sea, cool and salty, and the awnings begin to flap. This is when the flower seller packs her most delicate stems first, the tulips and the sweet peas, and leaves the sturdier sunflowers until last. She has learned the weather of the square the way a sailor learns the weather of a bay, by watching it every day and remembering what happened the last time the sky looked like this.

The last ferry of the day leaves at 18:05. On summer evenings the deck is full of people coming back from the island with sunburned shoulders and bags of shells. On winter evenings there may be only three or four passengers, wrapped in coats, watching the lights of the harbour grow larger as the boat approaches. The captain has made this crossing more than eleven thousand times. He says he still enjoys it, especially the moment when the engine slows and the harbour wall slides past close enough to touch.

## Closing time

When the market closes, the street changes again. The stalls are folded away, the carts roll back to the vans, and a man with a long broom sweeps the square from one end to the other. He hums while he works, always the same tune, and if you ask him what it is he will tell you he made it up when he was nine years old and never found a reason to stop. The pigeons wait at a safe distance until he has finished, then return to look for crumbs he has missed.

Marta closes her notebook and counts the coins in her tin. She does not count quickly. She likes the weight of them in her hand, and she likes the small ritual of stacking them by size: the large coins first, then the middle ones, then the smallest ones that nobody wants in their change. When the stacks are done, she writes the total in her notebook, underlines it twice, and puts the notebook back in her apron pocket.

The radio repairers lock the workshop at seven. Before they leave, they switch on the old set from 1957 one more time, just to hear it warm up. The waltz is gone, replaced by the evening news, but the sound is the same: soft at first, then clearer, as if the radio needs a moment to remember how to speak. They listen for a minute without saying anything, and then they turn it off, close the door, and walk home along the water.

The student with the card reader stays a little later. She sits on the harbour wall with her laptop open, exporting the day's payments and checking that every number adds up. When it does, she closes the laptop and watches the last light leave the water. Tomorrow there will be more honey to sell, more coffee to drink and, if she is lucky, no errors at the top of the screen.

By nine o'clock the square is quiet. The plane trees move gently in the wind, the ferry rests against its moorings, and the board beside the ticket office still shows the same five departures it has shown for years. In the morning the bakers will come again with their carts, and the smell of bread will reach the water before the light, and the market will begin as it always does, one stall at a time.

That is the end of the listening passage.
